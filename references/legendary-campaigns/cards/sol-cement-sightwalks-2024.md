# Sightwalks (Sol Cement · 2024)

## Insight
Universal tactile paving tells visually impaired people two things only: move forward, or stop. It gets them down the street but tells them nothing about what is around them — so to find a bank, a pharmacy or a restaurant they must depend on constant assistance from strangers. For a community already marginalized by lack of government support, that dependence is a daily loss of autonomy. And the entire system is made of one material: cement.

## Mechanic
Sol Cement designed a new tactile vocabulary that extends the universal system rather than replacing it. A horizontal bar on the sidewalk signals a coded tile where vertical lines can be counted with a cane: one line means restaurant, two means bank, three grocery store, four drugstore, and so on. The system was developed over almost two years with the active participation of visually impaired users testing every iteration, then matrix models were mass-produced and installed across 75,000 m2 of Miraflores — Peru's busiest commercial district — in two weeks. The country's blind organizations trained their members on the code, the district government inaugurated it publicly, and the designs and patents were released open and free so any city in the world can replicate them. An estimated 500,000+ people stand to benefit.

## Why it worked
P17 (Design as Idea): the entire campaign is a design object — there is no film to explain because the sidewalk is the message. P14 (Product as Proof): only a cement company could credibly ship this; the brand's product literally becomes the medium of inclusion, making "Sol builds more inclusive cement solutions" a demonstrated fact rather than a claim. P04 (Brand as Activist): by fixing what the government hadn't, Sol reopened the national conversation on inclusive cities from a position of authorship, not commentary. The low-tech nature is a feature — counting lines with a cane requires no app, no battery, no cost to the user, which is exactly why it can scale to every city.

## Steal
Find the universal standard in your category that everyone accepts as finished — then ask what vocabulary it is missing. Extending an existing behavior (tap the cane, count the lines) beats inventing a new one, because adoption rides on habits people already trust. And if the goal is category leadership, open-source the fix: ownership of the idea outlasts ownership of the patent.
