# Lil Sugar — Master of Disguise (Hip Hop Public Health · 2021)

## Insight
Excess sugar drives child obesity and diabetes in the US, and access to healthy food is a racial and health-equity issue. The industry's trick: added sugar hides in packaged food under more than 150 obscure scientific names — maltose, maltodextrin, all sugar. Health literacy lectures bore kids into ignoring them. So the campaign stole the food industry's own playbook: deliver the message disguised as something kids already love. To fight big sugar, create a Lil Sugar.

## Mechanic
Sugar was personified as Lil Sugar, a larger-than-life, mischievous animated villain who — "MTV Cribs" style — brags about his master plan of disguising himself under 150 names to sneak into food. Darryl "DMC" McDaniels of Run-DMC co-wrote and performed the hip hop track. The music video (distributed via donated pre-roll, movie theater screenings, social and community events) drives to the Lil Sugar app, which gamifies label literacy: point your camera at any packaged food to reveal which sugar character hides inside, collect the sugars, battle friends — Pokémon Go meets public health. A storybook extends it to younger kids as shared parent-child learning. Launched October 2021: 39,423 app downloads from community events, 5,852 schools registered, 3,089,107 students participating, NYC Board of Education endorsement.

## Why it worked
P02 (Enemy or Conflict): abstract "added sugar" becomes a nameable, taunting villain — you cannot fight 150 chemical names, but a kid can absolutely fight Lil Sugar. Giving the enemy charisma made vigilance feel like a game, not a lecture. P05 (Cultural Hijack): hip hop is the native language of the audience and carries built-in authority through DMC — health education arrives wearing the culture's own clothes, the exact mirror of how the industry disguises sugar in kid-friendly packaging. The scavenger-hunt app converts one-time awareness into a repeatable checking habit at the shelf — the actual behavior that matters.

## Steal
When your enemy is diffuse (a category, an ingredient, a systemic practice), condense it into one character with a face, a voice, and an ego — then let the audience win against it. And if the opposition succeeds by disguise, teach through un-masking: make detection itself the game.
