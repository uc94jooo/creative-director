# See Sound (Wavio · 2019)

## Insight
Five percent of the world's population lives with disabling hearing loss, and hearing is what drives situational awareness — the innate sense of what's happening around you. The case opens with a Deaf woman cooking while her friend's daughter fell down the stairs and cried out for help: "I had no idea what was happening." Existing assistive products either amplify sound (cochlear implants) or flash for one single trigger (doorbell, smoke alarm); nothing can distinguish a microwave from a baby crying from glass breaking. The blocker was never imagination — it was data: training a model to differentiate sounds requires millions of labelled samples no one had. Except the answer was in plain sight: YouTube's billion hours of video are full of labelled sound.

## Mechanic
See Sound is the world's first smart-home hearing system for the Deaf. Working with Google's AudioSet, the team trained a machine-learning model on over 2 million human-labelled 10-second sound clips sourced from YouTube — thousands of samples per sound type, from household noises to life-or-death alerts. Plug-in units connect via WiFi; when one of 75 recognised sounds occurs, the nearest unit interprets it, makes a confidence-scored prediction, and pushes a simple visual alert — duration, frequency, location — to the user's phone, designed for a community that relies on vision. Three patents and $160,000+ over four years led to a worldwide launch plan, plus a petition to have the device covered under the Americans with Disabilities Act, which would put it in millions of Deaf homes at no cost to users.

## Why it worked
P18 (Tech as Canvas): the creative leap is recognising YouTube as the world's largest accidental sound-labelling project — repurposing a public entertainment archive as medical training data. P14 (Product as Proof): a health agency's answer to an awareness problem is a working, patented device; the demo is the argument. P06 (Limitation as Power): built entirely around how Deaf people already navigate the world (visually), not around restoring what's missing — sound rendered as data to see rather than a deficit to fix. The ADA petition extends the idea from product to policy, aiming at systemic distribution instead of retail.

## Steal
When a product idea stalls on "we don't have the data," look for a public archive generating that data as a by-product of something else — the internet's exhaust is full of unlabelled training sets. And design for the ability your audience has in abundance, not the one they lack.
