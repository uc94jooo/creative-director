# You Can't Stop Us (Nike · 2020)

## Insight
The first half of 2020 was tumultuous: the world was fighting two pandemics — COVID-19 and racial injustice — while political divisions tore America apart. Sporting events were postponed or cancelled, and sport felt like the last thing on people's minds. Nike knew sport couldn't cure or save anyone, but its power has always been to guide, inspire, and unite — and at the moment the world was most divided, a reminder of what's possible "when we come back together" was exactly what sport could still offer.

## Mechanic
A split-screen film pairs different athletes mid-action so precisely that their combined movements read as one continuous body — a runner becomes a wheelchair racer, a swimmer becomes a surfer. The story arc moves from individual struggle through cancelled seasons and empty stadiums to protest and collective return, narrated by Megan Rapinoe: "when things aren't fair, we'll come together for change... because nothing can stop what we can do together." To land 36 perfectly matched scenes, the team researched over 4,000 sequences and attempted 900 pairings. The film ignited the news cycle around the NBA's return: 632.4M global impressions in the first 72 hours, 59M+ views on YouTube alone, 1,300+ athletes and sports organizations sharing it, and a 35-to-1 positive sentiment ratio.

## Why it worked
P08 (Craft as Message): the split-screen is not decoration — the seam *is* the thesis. Two different bodies moving as one literally performs the message of unity across race, gender, sport, and ability; the obsessive matching (4,000 sequences reviewed) is what makes the metaphor feel inevitable rather than clever. P04 (Brand as Activist): naming racial injustice alongside the pandemic extended the Kaepernick-era stance, so the film read as conviction, not opportunism. The Tier 3 emotion — defiance voiced from a moment of collective vulnerability — matched the audience's actual 2020 state better than either pure grief or pure optimism could.

## Steal
When your message is a relationship between two things (unity, contrast, before/after), build the relationship into the film's formal grammar instead of the script — let the editing technique embody the argument. And in a crisis, match emotional register precisely: acknowledge the wound first, then pivot to collective agency; skipping the wound reads as denial.
