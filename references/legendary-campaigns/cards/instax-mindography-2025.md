# [Mind]ography (Instax · 2025)

## Insight
57% of young Australians have lost a cherished digital photo with no physical backup — Instax's entire brand promise is "make the photo real before it's gone," but the most devastating version of that loss is a memory that was never even photographed in the first place.

## Mechanic
Instax worked with Princeton researcher Dr. Paul Scotti to reconstruct a real person's lost memories using their own brain activity. Nicole Toum, a Sydney support worker who lost her father — and, separately, dozens of digital photos of him — in 2018, had her brain scanned via fMRI while she viewed childhood photos and recalled specific memories. Machine learning trained on that brain-activity data generated visual reconstructions of three memories Nicole chose to recover, which were then printed as real, physical photographs on an Instax Mini Link 3 — turning a memory that existed only in her mind into an object she could hold.

## Why it worked
P18 (Tech as Canvas): fMRI-to-image reconstruction is used here for the most literal possible expression of the brand's function — Instax makes the intangible tangible, and this is that promise pushed to its technical limit. P08 (Craft as Message): the emotional stakes (a grieving daughter, memories of her father) only earn attention because the underlying neuroscience is genuinely rigorous, not simulated — the craft of the research is what makes the printed photo feel earned rather than gimmicky. Won Silver Spike in Digital Craft: AI Storytelling, Spikes Asia 2025.

## Steal
If your product's core function is "making something intangible real," find the most extreme, technically legitimate version of that transformation you can credibly execute — and anchor it in one specific, true human story rather than a hypothetical demo, so the technology reads as care rather than spectacle.
