# Hope (Red Cross / ICRC · 2018)

## Insight
Attacks on hospitals and healthcare workers are a daily occurrence in armed conflict, but the public registers them as statistics about infrastructure. The invisible truth is that the victims are not only the healthcare personnel — they are entire civilian populations who die, are maimed, or fall fatally ill after being deprived of the doctors and health centres where they would have sought care. When a hospital is bombed, everyone who would ever have needed it becomes a casualty. The film makes that abstraction unbearable by giving it one face: a wounded little girl whose survival depends on a building still standing.

## Mechanic
A nearly two-minute short film for the ICRC's "Healthcare is #NotATarget" campaign. Raya, a little girl in the back seat of her father's car, is bleeding from an abdominal injury as he races through a war-torn countryside. The entire film lives in his voice: he calms her, calls her his hero, begs her to stay awake — "don't sleep, don't sleep, open your eyes, sing me a song" — promising they're almost there. When they arrive, the hospital has been bombed. Title: "No hospitals. No hope." A closing plea states that healthcare workers are attacked every day in war zones. Produced with feature-film craft by Sra. Rushmore and Blur Films Madrid, it won the Film Craft Grand Prix at Cannes.

## Why it worked
P16 (Long-form Drama): the film's power is structural — it borrows the grammar of a rescue thriller (a race against time) and then withholds the genre's promised ending; the destination itself, not the journey, is what war has removed. P13 (Truth Telling): no metaphor, no softening — the campaign states plainly that hospitals are being targeted and lets a single indirect victim carry the global statistic. The father's composure is the emotional engine: he never breaks down on screen, so the audience must break down for him. By aiming at "those responsible for actions taking place in wars," the campaign uses public awareness as leverage on military and political actors — the audience's horror is the pressure mechanism.

## Steal
To make an invisible category of victim visible, dramatize one absence, not many casualties: show what should have been there. Borrowing a familiar film genre and amputating its resolution weaponizes the audience's own expectations. And when the subject is unbearable, give the on-screen character composure — restraint on screen produces collapse in the viewer.
