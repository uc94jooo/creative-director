# Real Tone (Google · 2022)

## Insight
Camera technology has excluded people of colour by design: film cameras were calibrated against the "Shirley Card" — colour swatches beside a photo of a white woman — so darker skin has been mis-rendered for generations ("every single yearbook photo of mine has been terribly shot since I was a kid"). The launch stage sharpened the insight: the Super Bowl is America's biggest and whitest advertising stage — 76% of characters in 2022 Super Bowl ads had the three lightest Fitzpatrick skin tones, and 66% of dark-skinned consumers feel stereotypically portrayed in ads. Pixel 6's Real Tone technology — the first camera intended to make imaging genuinely work for people of colour — deserved a launch that was itself an act of representation: everyone deserves to be seen as they truly are.

## Mechanic
A 60-second Super Bowl 2022 spot ("Seen on Pixel") opened with testimonials from people of colour who had struggled to be properly represented in their own photographs, then showcased 40+ real portraits shot on Pixel 6 across ranges of skin tones, races and cultures — in their real tone — scored by Lizzo premiering her unreleased track "If You Love Me" ("if you love me, you love all of me"). Representation extended behind the camera: a deliberately diverse team of photographers, cinematographers, colorists, retouchers and casting. The film closed on "Everyone deserves to be seen as they truly are," with social cutdowns, interactive posts and influencer amplification through Q1. Results: Pixel 6 searches up 86x after airing, Google Store traffic +400% on Super Bowl Sunday, +7% ad memorability and +12.5% brand memorability vs. the average Super Bowl spot, +50% brand lift among Pixel advocates, +25% purchase intent. Mobile Grand Prix at Cannes 2022.

## Why it worked
P13 (Truth Telling): the campaign named an uncomfortable historical truth — the racial bias built into camera calibration itself — and let real people testify to it, giving a phone launch the weight of a cultural correction. P14 (Product as Proof): every claim was demonstrated, not asserted; the beautiful portraits are the product working, so the ad couldn't be dismissed as diversity posturing — the camera either renders melanin truthfully or it doesn't. The media choice was the message: placing the darkest-skinned cast of the night into the whitest ad environment in America made the spot's context part of its argument, and matching on-screen representation with behind-camera representation protected it from accusations of skin-deep inclusion.

## Steal
If your product corrects an injustice, let the product perform the correction on the biggest stage where the injustice is most visible — the contrast with surrounding context multiplies the message. And audit the making, not just the casting: when the campaign is about representation, who is behind the camera is part of the creative idea.
