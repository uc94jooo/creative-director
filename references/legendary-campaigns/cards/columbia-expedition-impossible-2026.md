# Expedition Impossible (Columbia Sportswear · 2026)

## Insight
Outdoor brands all claim to understand the planet's terrain better than anyone — a claim that's impossible to prove and easy to ignore. The flat-earth movement, meanwhile, is a small but genuinely persistent community making an extraordinary, disprovable claim about that same terrain. Nobody had ever forced the two claims to collide with something real at stake.

## Mechanic
Columbia CEO Tim Boyle published an open letter in The New York Times and across social channels, personally challenging flat-earth believers to physically locate and photograph the edge of the planet. The prize: full ownership of Columbia Sportswear, a $3 billion company, warehouses and office furniture included. The rules were precise and public — no ordinary cliff-top, no cul-de-sac, no optical illusion would qualify, only genuine proof of a literal edge — which both made the offer credible as a real challenge and ensured it could never actually be won through honest submission. The open letter rolled out into a wider video and social campaign; Boyle says he still gets emails from people wanting to participate.

## Why it worked
P02 (Enemy or Conflict): rather than arguing against flat-earth belief in the abstract, Columbia gave the argument a physical, disprovable form and staked something real on the outcome, converting a fringe online debate into a mainstream news story with genuine stakes attached. P06 (Limitation as Power): the campaign's core resource is the fact that the challenge is structurally impossible to complete — the brand's confidence in geography becomes the entire creative engine, no other execution needed. The bet was won without ever being at real financial risk (no valid submission could exist), yet the CEO's public willingness to make it read as sincere rather than cynical, converting into 10 Cannes Lions in 2026 (Grand Prix in Brand Experience & Activation, the Dan Wieden Titanium Lion, 2 Gold, 3 Silver, 3 Bronze), 80 million campaign views, and a credited role in a 35% sales surge in Europe.

## Steal
Find the one claim your category makes that's theoretically falsifiable but nobody has ever actually tested publicly, then design a real, precisely-ruled challenge around it with a stake big enough to be newsworthy — the specificity of the rules is what makes an obviously safe bet still read as brave.
