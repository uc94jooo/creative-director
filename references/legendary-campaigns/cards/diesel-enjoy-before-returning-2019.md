# Enjoy Before Returning (Diesel · 2019)

## Insight
Wardrobing — buying clothes, wearing them once with the tags on, and returning them within 14 days for a full refund — costs the fashion industry $15 billion a year. One in five shoppers does it. Retailers respond by blacklisting serial returners, and it changes nothing: the behavior is embedded in a generation's DNA. Diesel's read: if nothing can stop people from wardrobing, stop trying to stop them — the brand that sides with the "immoral" behavior owns the conversation every competitor is losing.

## Mechanic
For the FW19 launch, Diesel made "Enjoy Before Returning" the campaign platform. The return policy became the hero of the global communication — sung in the launch film, slapped across fashion visuals of models unapologetically wearing tags out while planning their returns. Timing hijacked global Fashion Week, when wardrobing spikes; Diesel parties admitted only people wearing labels out, any brand. Photos of yourself in Diesel with the tag out counted as valid discount coupons on ecommerce. Outcome: returns actually fell 9% globally, sales rose 24% globally, and over half of that growth came from young new customers who had never considered Diesel. Grand Prix at Cannes.

## Why it worked
P03 (Behavior Inversion): the category punishes the behavior; Diesel celebrates it — and paradoxically the permission kills the thrill, cutting returns. P05 (Cultural Hijack): Fashion Week, the peak wardrobing moment, becomes the media plan, and the visible tag becomes a wearable brand asset. P13 (Truth Telling): naming an embarrassing consumer truth out loud is what earned young shoppers' respect — Diesel positioned itself as the one brand honest enough to admit how people actually shop. Classic Diesel brand behavior: taking the industry's shame and wearing it as style.

## Steal
Find the "fraudulent" or embarrassing consumer behavior your category spends money fighting, and ask what happens if you legitimize it instead. Prohibition fuels the behavior; permission often deflates it — and the first brand to grant permission harvests all the cultural credit. Bonus test: can the evidence of the behavior (the tag) become your logo's new home?
