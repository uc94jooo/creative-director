# Scrolling Therapy (Eurofarma · 2023)

## Insight
An early symptom of Parkinson's is facial masking: patients lose the ability to smile, laugh or look surprised, which devastates relationships and wellbeing. A daily 45-minute facial exercise routine significantly improves symptoms — but only 3% of patients follow it, because it is exhausting, frustrating, and requires watching yourself in a mirror when your face no longer follows your heart. Meanwhile, everyone — patients included — already spends 2.5 hours a day scrolling social feeds. The unlock: convert down time into therapy time, and remove the mirror from the routine entirely.

## Mechanic
Scrolling Therapy is a mobile app that uses facial recognition to browse the news feed. Built on Google's open-source TensorFlow platform, it reads 10,000+ data points across 43 facial muscles through the device camera and converts the most therapeutically important facial exercises into social media commands: smile = like, surprised face = scroll down, disgusted face = scroll up, sad face = play, angry face = swipe. Real-time visual feedback confirms each exercise is performed correctly — so the feed itself replaces the mirror, tackling both the practical and emotional barriers to adherence. The exercise also generates biofeedback: expressing an emotion intensifies feeling it, improving brain activity. Launched across Latin America with official support from the American Parkinson Disease Association and Brazil's Parkinson's Association, available worldwide to 8.5M+ patients. Won the Pharma Grand Prix at Cannes 2023.

## Why it worked
P03 (Behavior Inversion): instead of asking patients to add a gruelling new routine, it hijacked an existing compulsive behaviour — scrolling — and made the therapy invisible inside it. The 3%-adherence problem was never about awareness; it was about friction and emotional pain, and the idea deleted both. P18 (Tech as Canvas): facial recognition wasn't a gimmick layered onto an ad — the technology is the entire product, purpose-matched to a disease defined by facial muscles. The design detail matters: replacing the mirror with the feed shows deep patient empathy (validated through neurologist and physiotherapist interviews and journey mapping), and turning expressions into rewards (a smile literally "likes" the world) gives the mechanic poetic coherence.

## Steal
When adherence is the problem, don't motivate harder — relocate the desired behaviour inside a behaviour people already can't stop doing. Map the patient's (or user's) day, find the largest block of automatic behaviour, and ask what your therapy, training or habit could piggyback on with zero added willpower.
