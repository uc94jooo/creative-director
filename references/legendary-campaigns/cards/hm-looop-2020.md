# Looop (H&M · 2020)

## Insight
People recycle glass, PET bottles and newspapers without a second thought — yet 87% of clothing globally ends up as landfill. The gap isn't intention, it's the intention-to-action gap: recycling clothes is abstract, delayed and invisible, so old garments read as waste rather than resource. H&M — the brand that democratised fast fashion and therefore carries its responsibility — needed circularity to move from sustainability report to retail experience: something customers could watch happen to their own clothes, then and there, with immediate gratification.

## Mechanic
Looop is the world's first in-store garment-to-garment recycling system, developed with the Hong Kong Research Institute of Textiles and Apparel and the H&M Foundation, installed in a glass box in H&M's Stockholm flagship. Customers bring an old garment, use an app to select a size and one of eight ready-to-wear designs, and watch Looop shred their garment into fibres, spin it into yarn and knit it into something new — without water or chemicals — right in front of their eyes. The space was designed as a creative atelier celebrating process over product: eight giant floor-balanced screens depict the eight recycling steps as animated macro loops with ASMR sound showers; a striking red yarn installation (H&M's signature red as literal red thread) weaves through the store as a wayfinder into the remake space; a website carries the story globally. Despite launching in October 2020 under Covid restrictions, remake slots booked out four months in advance within weeks; 500+ articles across 55 markets and 15M+ social reach followed. HKRITA licensed the technology to the whole industry. Grand Prix at Cannes.

## Why it worked
P14 (Product as Proof): H&M didn't claim circularity — it built the machine and put it on the shop floor, converting a sustainability promise into a witnessable physical process; the glass box is the argument. P08 (Craft as Message): the atelier design language, macro-animated process art and ASMR soundscape aestheticise recycling itself, reframing "waste processing" as craftsmanship — which is exactly the mental shift ("not waste but resource") the campaign needed to produce. Immediate gratification closed the intention-action gap: your garment, your choice of design, transformed before your eyes. And licensing the technology industry-wide turned a flagship stunt into category leadership rather than a one-store PR play.

## Steal
To change a behaviour stuck at the intention-action gap, collapse the distance between the good deed and its visible result — make the outcome immediate, personal and watchable. And when your brand is part of the problem, the credible move is infrastructure, not messaging: build the fix into the retail experience and open-license it so the industry must follow.
